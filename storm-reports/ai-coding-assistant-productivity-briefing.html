The 60-second summary

Settled: AI coding assistants produce real, measured output gains in specific contexts — scoped/greenfield tasks and less-experienced developers. The strongest evidence is a peer-reviewed set of three field RCTs (4,867 developers) showing GitHub Copilot raised completed tasks ~26%, with larger gains for juniors.

Contested: whether they help experienced developers in large, mature codebases. The only randomized trial on that population (METR, 16 devs, a 2025 snapshot) found a 19% slowdown — while those same developers believed they were ~20% faster. The robust, cross-cutting finding is therefore not a speed number at all: it is the perception–reality gap — self-reported productivity is a biased estimator of actual output, so any org "measuring" AI ROI via developer surveys is measuring belief, not work.

Key findings — ranked by reliability

01

For typical/junior devs on scoped tasks, AI raises task throughput ~26%

confidence 8/10
Supported by: Academic, Practitioner, Economist Challenged by: Skeptic (counts completion, not quality)

Three field RCTs, 4,867 developers (Microsoft/Accenture/Fortune-100), peer-reviewed in Management Science: +26.1% completed tasks (SE ~10pp), larger for less-experienced devs. Measures task-completion count, not code quality, correctness, or maintainability — the known blind spot.

02

Developers' felt speedup is decoupled from their measured output (the perception gap)

confidence 8/10
Supported by: all five lenses Challenged by: — (direction robust; magnitude is time-specific)

METR's RCT: experienced devs were 19% slower with AI yet forecast +24% and reported +20% after the fact — a ~39-point gap, inside one randomized study. The direction (belief ≠ output) is the single most replicated, load-bearing result here. Caveat: small-n preprint and a 2025 snapshot — METR's Feb-2026 follow-up found late-2025 tools improved to a positive effect, so the −19% is a moment-in-time number, not an evergreen verdict.

03

The gains are context-split: greenfield/novel/junior win; experienced devs in mature repos go flat-to-negative

confidence 7/10
Supported by: Practitioner, Academic, Historian Challenged by: vendor single-number claims

The apparent +26% vs −19% conflict dissolves once you condition on developer expertise and codebase familiarity. AI supplies context the novice lacks; it cannot supply the tacit context an expert already holds — so it helps most exactly where the hype is weakest and helps least exactly where the hype is loudest.

04

Individual time-savings don't propagate to org-level delivery

confidence 6/10
Supported by: Practitioner, Skeptic, Economist Challenged by: vendor adoption stats

Google's DORA 2024 estimated that +25% AI adoption was associated with −1.5% delivery throughput and −7.2% stability (2025: throughput turned positive, stability still negative). Observational survey, not causal — DORA frames it as AI amplifying existing team strengths/weaknesses, with the bottleneck shifting to review/QA.

05

"Saved" coding time relocates to review, debugging, and maintenance — and quality/security can decay

confidence 6/10
Supported by: Skeptic, Practitioner Challenged by: completion-only metrics

NYU/IEEE S&P (peer-reviewed): ~40% of Copilot completions across 1,689 programs contained a security weakness. GitClear (commercial, 211M lines): copy-pasted code rose 8.3%→12.3% and overtook refactored code for the first time. The mechanism the speed-numbers ignore: effort moves from typing to reviewing plausible-but-subtly-wrong code, which is slower and more draining when you already know the codebase.

The hidden connection

The pricing model explains the measurement gap. AI coding tools are sold per seat, so vendor revenue scales with adoption and retention — and the cheapest driver of both is the feeling of speed, which the evidence shows is decoupled from real output. That is why every billion-dollar incumbent's "productivity" proof is internally produced on trivial greenfield tasks (where AI genuinely wins), while the one study engineered to be unsellable — independent funding, real repos, experienced devs — is the one that found a loss. The perception gap isn't just a psychology curiosity; it is the business model's load-bearing asset.

Key assumption · the missing 6th lens

The long-term maintainer / code archaeologist. Every metric here is short-horizon: task time, completion count, first-draft volume. None measures the multi-year cost — maintainability debt as AI-written code accumulates, the review burden compounding, and skill atrophy in juniors who ship working code without ever learning the hard parts. The 6th lens asks the question that would change the conclusion: what is the 3-year total cost of ownership of AI-accelerated code? No study here answers it.

What to do — for an org curating the Crucible (+ inspeximus / RAMR)

  • Crucible candidate (high value): the canonical "GitHub Copilot makes devs 55.8% faster" stat is a vendor preprint on a single greenfield JS HTTP-server task, ~95 recruited / ~70 completers — fragile as a universal claim. Pair it against the independent METR −19% (experienced/mature repos) as a "vendor benchmark vs independent RCT — context flips the sign" ledger entry. The FAILED reading is the universal "X% faster" claim, not AI coding itself.
  • External anchor for our overconfidence / calibration line (cite, don't re-run): the METR perception gap (−19% measured, +20% felt) is a real-world human instance of the exact theme our overconfidence-tax and LLM-judge probes measure in models — self-report is a biased estimator of true performance. Strong, verified corroboration for that canon.
  • Methods receipt (eval-folklore Crucible): the +26% vs −19% "contradiction" is the same shape as our LLM-judge / Arena work — the metric isn't measuring what it claims. Completion-count ≠ time-to-done ≠ downstream defect cost; the sign depends on which you pick and on whom. A clean "measure-what-matters" receipt.
  • inspeximus / RAMR hook: experienced devs lose in mature repos precisely because the AI lacks the deep context they hold — the binding constraint is context/memory quality, not raw model capability. That is the argument for a good agent-memory layer (inspeximus = supply the context the model lacks) and a contamination-resistant memory benchmark (RAMR). And "individual gain doesn't reach the org because the bottleneck shifts to verification" is the verification-bottleneck theme in the wild.
  • Discipline: never publish a single number as "the" productivity effect. The honest claim is conditional: population × task type × time horizon.

Claim safety guide

Safe to assert

  • Gains are real but context-dependent (juniors/greenfield up; experts/mature repos flat-to-negative).
  • The perception–reality gap is real and replicated in direction (METR).
  • Peer-reviewed RCT: +26% task completion for typical devs (Cui/Demirer, Management Science).
  • ~40% of Copilot completions had a security weakness (NYU, peer-reviewed).

Assert with caveat

  • METR −19%: small-n preprint and a 2025 snapshot — pair with the Feb-2026 uplift revision.
  • +26% is completion-count, not quality/maintainability.
  • DORA −7.2% stability: observational survey, not causal.
  • Cursor ~$50B / Copilot ~$1B ARR: reported/analyst-estimated, not all company-disclosed.

Avoid / don't repeat

  • "AI makes developers X% more productive" as a universal claim.
  • The vendor "55.8% faster" as representative of real work.
  • GitClear churn "doubled" — it was projected to; commercial, not peer-reviewed.
  • Citing developer-survey self-reports as evidence of actual output.

Contested signal (demoted in verification)

METR −19% — striking and confirmed, but n=16 preprint and a 2025-snapshot; the Feb-2026 follow-up shows the gap narrowed with newer tools. Use for the direction (perception gap), not the evergreen magnitude. Peng "55.8% faster" — real number, but a GitHub/Microsoft preprint on one toy task with an attrition-reduced sample; not representative. GitClear code-quality decay — commercial report (not peer-reviewed); copy-paste rise is measured, but "churn doubled" was a projection.

The frontier question

Does AI help or hurt experienced developers in mature codebases — measured by time-to-merged plus downstream defect and maintenance cost over 6–12 months — in a large, pre-registered, peer-reviewed RCT? No such study exists; METR (n=16, preprint) is the only randomized evidence on that population.
