The 60-second summary

Roth (2022, AER: Insights, published) is real and correctly characterized: standard pre-trends tests have low power against realistic violations, and conditioning inference on passing the test makes things worse, not better. That part of the post is solid. Where the panel pushed back hard, converging from two independent directions (Skeptic and Academic): the post's specific headline numbers — a gentle drift biases the estimate by 77%, detected only 16% of the time — are the org's OWN simulation output from one stylized single-treated-unit design, not figures Roth himself reported (his calibration used a survey of real published multi-unit designs). This is a real, fixable precision issue, not a fatal flaw — the qualitative mechanism reproduces faithfully; the specific numbers just needed to stop reading as if quoted from Roth. A second, genuine methodological wrinkle: a single-treated-unit DiD design requires an extra assumption (homoskedastic errors across units, Conley & Taber 2011) for valid inference, which this post's simulation imposes by construction rather than earning empirically — worth disclosing. The practical stakes are real: the field's most widely used applied DiD toolkit doesn't even cite Roth or mention test power, and historically (per the Historian lens), proof that a diagnostic is broken rarely changes field-wide practice on its own.

The hidden connection

The Skeptic/Academic precision issue and the Practitioner's applied-gap finding are two sides of the same coin: the reason a single-treated-unit toy simulation is tempting to build (and to publish numbers from) is exactly the reason practitioners don't run power checks in real applied work — the honest power-calibration Roth actually did (a survey of real heterogeneous designs) is expensive and doesn't reduce to one quotable number, while a stylized simulation does. The org fell into a milder version of the same trap the field's applied tooling falls into: a clean, quotable number is more attractive than an honestly-scoped, harder-to-summarize truth.

Key assumption · the missing 6th lens

None of the five lenses examined how test power scales with the SPECIFIC violation shape assumed. The post (and Roth) test linear drift; a regime-shift, mean-reverting wobble, or discrete jump might be substantially easier or harder for a pre-trends test to catch, and applied panels rarely know in advance which shape their own violation (if any) would take. A "shape-robustness" lens — sweeping violation functional forms, not just magnitude — would sharpen exactly how general the "16% detection" number is.

What to do — for applied economists and causal-inference practitioners

  • Don't treat "passed the pre-trends test" as clearance, especially with 6 or fewer pre-periods — run Roth's pretrends package (slope_for_power()) against a plausible violation size for YOUR specific design before trusting a pass.
  • If your DiD design has one or very few treated units, check whether you're implicitly assuming homoskedastic errors across units (Conley & Taber 2011) — if that assumption is shaky, standard inference may not be valid regardless of what the pre-trends test says.
  • Report sensitivity bounds (Rambachan & Roth's "honest DiD") for the plausible range of violations your power analysis can't rule out, rather than a single pass/fail p-value.
  • Treat "the correct test detects 16% of realistic violations" as this org's own simulation-specific number, not a citable fact from Roth (2022) — cite Roth for the mechanism, this org's Lab for the illustrative number.

  • Callaway's widely-used did package documentation doesn't cite Roth 2022 or discuss test power — a real, checkable applied-tooling gap.

  • "77% bias / 16% detection" — assert only as this org's own simulation output for one specific single-treated-unit design, not as Roth's reported figures.
  • Mikhaeil & Harshaw (2025) contest the "underpowered" framing itself — real, but an unpublished preprint with a genuinely unresolved counter-argument, not a settled rebuttal.

Avoid / don't repeat

  • Do NOT cite "NBER w31666 finds design-based methods in 72.6% of empirical economics articles" — this specific figure could not be located anywhere in that paper and appears fabricated or citation-swapped. The paper is about false-rejection rates in t-statistic distributions, unrelated to method-adoption shares.

Contested signal

Mikhaeil & Harshaw, "In Defense of the Pre-Test" (arXiv:2510.26470, Oct 2025) — real preprint, correct argument (the "underpowered" framing implicitly tests against a zero-violation null nobody actually believes; they propose a conditional-extrapolation threshold instead). Not yet peer-reviewed; presents a genuinely live, unresolved methodological disagreement with Roth's framing, not a refutation.

The frontier question

Does pre-trends test power against realistic drifts vary substantially by VIOLATION SHAPE (linear vs. regime-shift vs. mean-reverting), not just magnitude and panel length — and if so, which shape is most representative of real applied violations, so power calculations stop defaulting to the mathematically convenient linear case?
